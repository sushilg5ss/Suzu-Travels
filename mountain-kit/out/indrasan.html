What is Indrasan, and how do you climb it?

Indrasan (6,220 m / 20,407 ft) is a peak of the Pir Panjal range above Manali, in Kullu district, Himachal Pradesh, right beside Deo Tibba. It is called the hardest peak of the Pir Panjal: icefalls, steep ice and rock bands guard every side. Modern teams approach from Jagatsukh through the Duhangan valley; the best months are May–June and September–October. See all the 6,000 m peaks of India.

Seen from the Kullu valley, Deo Tibba is the broad white dome and Indrasan the sharper summit beside it. The two peaks share a high snow plateau and the Duhangan Col, and for most of the approach their routes are the same. Above the plateau they part ways: Deo Tibba is a long snow plod to a flat top, while Indrasan rises in steep ice and rock on every side. Wikipedia calls it the most difficult mountain to climb in the Pir Panjal range, and its name means the royal seat of Lord Indra.

This page sets out the routes with approximate camp altitudes, the honest difficulty, the season, the permits and the history from the failed 1958 attempt to the 1962 first ascent and the ridge routes of the 1970s. Route and camps

Route from Manali, camp by camp

Today most teams start like a Deo Tibba expedition: a short drive from Manali to Jagatsukh and the Khanol road-head, then a walk up the Duhangan valley past Chikka and the Seri meadow to a base camp at Tainta, below the Duhangan Col. Above an advance base, a couloir leads over the col to the snow plateau between Deo Tibba and Indrasan, at about 5,500 m (the "18,000-ft plateau" of the early accounts).

From the plateau the usual modern line is the West Ridge, first reached by a 1971 party. The first ascent in 1962 came from the other side: up the Parbati valley and the Malana glen to the Malana Glacier, through a 700 m icefall, and finally up the south face. Day by day

A typical plan (estimate, not a fixed schedule)

  1. Day 1 · ManaliArrive, check gear and paperwork, and settle in at 2,050 m.
  2. Days 2–4 · Walk-in to base campDrive to the Jagatsukh / Khanol road-head and walk up the Duhangan valley via Chikka and Seri to Tainta base camp.
  3. Days 5–7 · Base camp and advance baseRest, snow and ice training, and load carries to advance base at about 4,500 m.
  4. Days 8–10 · Over the colFix the couloir over the Duhangan Col and move up to high camp on the plateau, about 5,500 m.
  5. Day 11 · Summit dayBy the West Ridge to the 6,220 m summit, then back to high camp.
  6. Days 12–14+ · Spare days and walk-outKept for weather, a second attempt and a careful descent to Manali.

Itineraries vary with the outfitter, group and weather; rest days are added when needed.

What it takes

How hard is Indrasan?

Indrasan is graded technical and is often called the hardest peak of the Pir Panjal. Every route meets serious ground: the 1962 party climbed a 700 m icefall and a steep final face, the 1973 East Ridge team climbed rock up to Very Severe on fixed ropes, and the modern West Ridge from the plateau is still a technical rope-and-ice climb. It is not a beginner peak or a first 6,000er.

You should arrive with earlier technical climbs and solid rope and ice skills. A sensible step before Indrasan is Deo Tibba, on the same approach, or Hanuman Tibba. If you are earlier in your climbing, start with our guide to choosing a first 6,000 m peak, read our altitude sickness guide and compare mountaineering courses in India.

  • ExperienceEarlier technical climbs, ideally a 6,000 m summit such as Deo Tibba or a peak like Hanuman Tibba.
  • TechniqueFront-pointing on steep ice, moving on rock and mixed ground, fixed ropes, abseiling and crevasse rescue.
  • FitnessLoad carries over the Duhangan Col and a long summit day above 5,500 m.
  • AcclimatisationA gradual walk-in through Chikka and Seri, and rest days at base camp before the col.
  • TrainingA Basic and preferably an Advanced Mountaineering Course before an expedition like this.
When to go

Best time to climb Indrasan

May–June before the monsoon and September–October after it. July and August, the monsoon months, are left out. The historic climbs fit this pattern: the first ascent was made in mid-October 1962, and the 1973 East Ridge climb in early June.

Build in weather days: fresh snow loads the couloir over the col and makes the upper ridge slower and more dangerous.

Permits

Permits for Indrasan

Indian climbers need permission from the Indian Mountaineering Foundation (IMF) for an expedition. In Himachal, climbs go through an HP Tourism-registered outfitter with registered guides, with police registration and insurance for every participant under the state's adventure rules; difficult routes need a suitably qualified lead.

Foreign nationals can climb Indrasan with IMF permission, applied for at least 90 days ahead, and a liaison officer. At 6,220 m it falls in the up to 6,500 m band: a base fee of US$500 for a team of two, plus per-member charges and US$500 for liaison-officer equipment (IMF peak-fee page, checked Oct 2026). Indrasan is in Kullu district, not the border belt, so no protected-area permit is needed. More in our IMF permit and peak fee guide and our guide to mountaineering in India for foreign climbers.

History

Climbing history of Indrasan

In 1958 Bob Pettigrew's party came up the Duhangan side to try Deo Tibba and Indrasan, but storms and illness stopped them well short of Indrasan. The first ascent came on 13 October 1962, by Kojiro Tomita and Yasumasa Miyaki of the Kyoto University Alpine Club expedition led by Dr Konoshin Onodera. Coming up the Malana Glacier, they placed camps at about 4,500 m, 5,200 m and 5,500 m and a bivouac near 6,150 m, failed once on 11 October, then reached the top by the south face at about 4:30 pm. The same day their teammates Omori and Tanaka stood on Deo Tibba.

A 1971 party reached the West Ridge, now the usual line from the Duhangan side. In 1973 the British Army Mountaineering Association climbed the East Ridge, with rock up to Very Severe and fixed ropes; Owens, Anderson and Eskell reached the summit. Because the records are incomplete, we do not number the ascents.

Pir Panjal ladder

Indrasan and its Manali neighbours, side by side

Four summits above Manali make a natural ladder. Friendship Peak (5,289 m) is the classic first snow peak, Hanuman Tibba (5,982 m) is a steep technical pyramid, Deo Tibba (6,001 m) is the snow dome next door, and Indrasan (6,220 m) is the hardest of them all.

FAQ

Indrasan: questions people ask

How high is Indrasan?

6,220 m (20,407 ft) in our dataset. The Himalayan Journal and Wikipedia give 6,221 m.

Where is Indrasan?

In the Pir Panjal range above Manali, in Kullu district, Himachal Pradesh, right next to Deo Tibba.

Why is Indrasan so hard?

Icefalls, steep ice and rock bands guard every side. It is called the most difficult peak of the Pir Panjal range.

Who first climbed Indrasan?

Kojiro Tomita and Yasumasa Miyaki of the Kyoto University Alpine Club, on 13 October 1962, by the south face.

What does Indrasan mean?

"Indra" + "Aasana": the royal seat of Lord Indra.

Indrasan or Deo Tibba: what is the difference?

They share the Duhangan valley, the col and the high plateau. Deo Tibba (6,001 m) is a snow dome; Indrasan (6,220 m) is a technical rock-and-ice summit.

When is the best time to climb Indrasan?

May–June and September–October, either side of the monsoon.

Can beginners climb Indrasan?

No. It needs earlier technical climbs, such as Deo Tibba or Hanuman Tibba, and solid rope and ice skills.

Can foreigners climb Indrasan?

Yes, with IMF permission applied for at least 90 days ahead and a liaison officer. The base fee for peaks up to 6,500 m is US$500 for a team of two (Oct 2026), plus other charges.
