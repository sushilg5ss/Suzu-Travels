What do the main mountaineering terms mean?

Mountaineering terms describe the ground, the gear and the plan. A col is a pass between two summits, a serac a tower of glacier ice, a bergschrund the crevasse at a glacier's head. Alpine style means carrying everything yourself; grades run from F (easy) to ED. Below: 52 terms, with examples from our mountains of India guide.

Ice and snow

Glacier and snow terms: moraine to cornice

Glacier

A long-lived mass of ice and snow that flows slowly downhill under its own weight. Most Himalayan climbs cross one before the real climbing starts.

Moraine

Rock debris a glacier carries and dumps. Lateral moraine lines its sides, medial moraine runs down the middle where two glaciers join, and terminal moraine marks the furthest reach of its snout. Expect hours of boulder-hopping on it.

Crevasse

A crack in the surface of a moving glacier. Some are more than 30 m (100 ft) deep, which is why glacier teams travel roped together.

Snow bridge

Snow lying across a crevasse. It can hide the crack completely, and it may not hold your weight.

Bergschrund

The crevasse at the head of a glacier, where moving ice pulls away from the ice or rock above it. It is often the first real obstacle below a face, can be very deep, and is usually most open and dangerous in summer and autumn.

Serac

A tower or block of glacier ice, formed in icefalls and where crevasses meet. Seracs can collapse without warning. On Shivling the west ridge passes a serac barrier at about 6,100 m (20,000 ft).

Icefall

The place where a glacier drops over a steep bed and breaks up into crevasses and seracs: slow, careful ground.

Névé

Granular snow-ice in the upper part of a glacier, the zone where snow builds up.

Firn

Old, compacted snow on its way to becoming glacier ice.

Cornice

An overhanging lip of wind-blown snow on a ridge. It can break off under a climber, so walk well back from the edge, on the side it does not overhang.

Verglas

A thin, clear glaze of ice on rock. Hard to see and very slippery.

The shape of a mountain

Mountain features: col, arête, couloir and more

Arête

A narrow, sharp ridge, often between two glacier valleys or bowls.

Ridge

A high crest running out from a summit. Many normal routes follow one to the top.

Col

A high pass or saddle between two summits. Teams often camp or cross there: the Duhangan Col is the crux of Deo Tibba.

Couloir

A steep gully that holds snow or ice, often a natural line up a face.

Gully

A wide, shallow ravine on a mountainside.

Cirque

A bowl-shaped hollow at the head of a glacier valley, carved by the ice.

Horn

A pointed peak carved by three or more cirques eating into it from different sides.

Buttress

A prominent rock feature jutting out from a mountain face.

Gendarme

A rock tower standing on a ridge.

Headwall

The part of a face where it steepens sharply near the top. Meru Central (6,310 m / 20,702 ft) has an overhanging headwall from about 5,900 m.

Scree and talus

Scree is small loose stones that slide underfoot; talus is bigger blocks.

Rope, gear and technique

Climbing terms for rope, gear and technique

Anchor

A fixed set of protection that holds a belay, a top rope or an abseil.

Belay

Protecting a roped climber by controlling the rope, usually through a belay device, so a fall is held.

Pitch

One rope-length section of a climb between two belays, typically up to about 50 m (165 ft).

Fixed rope

A rope anchored in place on a slope. Climbers clip into it for safety, go up it with an ascender, or abseil down it.

Ascender (jumar)

A mechanical clamp for climbing up a fixed rope. Climbers often call it a jumar, after one type.

Abseil / rappel

Going down a rope fixed to an anchor, with a descender device that controls your speed.

Prusik

A friction knot that grips a rope when loaded and slides when not. Named after Karl Prusik (1931).

Aid climbing

Using gear to pull on or stand on for upward progress, not only as protection. The opposite of free climbing. Meru's Shark's Fin needed aid up to grade A4.

Crampons

Spiked frames fixed to boots for snow and ice. Which crampon fits which boot is in our guide to crampons and mountaineering boots.

Ice axe

The climber's tool with a pick, an adze and a spike: walking stick, anchor and brake in one. See the ice axe guide.

Self-arrest

Stopping a slide on snow by driving the pick of the ice axe into the slope. Practise it before you need it.

How the climb is run

Alpine style, expedition style and summit day

Alpine style

The team carries everything itself and climbs in one push, with no fixed ropes and no stocked camps.

Expedition (siege) style

Stocked camps, fixed ropes and support staff move climbers up the mountain step by step. The opposite of alpine style.

Alpine start

Leaving very early, often before 5 a.m., while the snow is firm and less rock is falling.

Summit push

The final climb from the top camp to the summit and back down, usually begun in the dark with an alpine start.

Base camp

The main camp at the foot of the climb. What it looks like on an Indian peak is in our guide to expedition camping.

Bivouac (bivvy)

A rough overnight stop on the route without a full camp; on big walls sometimes on a hanging portaledge.

Acclimatisation

The body's gradual adjustment to thinner air. Altitude illness becomes a risk from a sleeping height of about 2,450 m (8,000 ft); read the altitude sickness guide.

F to ED

Alpine grades: what F, PD, AD, D, TD and ED mean

Alpine routes are graded with French words, from easy to extremely difficult. The grade describes the route, not the mountain: one peak can have a moderate route and an extreme one, as Menthosa shows.

Permit words

Indian mountaineering terminology: IMF, LO, PAP, UKMPS, HAP

IMF

The Indian Mountaineering Foundation, the national body that permits foreign expeditions to Indian peaks. Fees are explained on our IMF permit fees page.

LO

Liaison Officer: the officer the IMF appoints to every foreign expedition. Teams pay a US$500 LO equipment hire on top of the peak fee (IMF, checked 1 Oct 2026).

PAP

Protected Area Permit, needed by foreigners in some border belts, for example the Pooh block of Kinnaur on the far side of Manirang.

UKMPS

The Uttarakhand Mountaineering Permission System: the state's online permit portal, new in 2026.

HAP

High-altitude porter: support staff on the mountain, whom the IMF requires to be insured.

Trekking peak

The IMF's lower-fee category (US$200 base for a team of two), for peaks on the IMF's own list only. More on our trekking peaks page.

The words on real peaks

Five Himachal peaks, from snow slope to alpine AD

Height and difficulty are not the same thing. Friendship Peak (5,289 m / 17,352 ft) is a classic beginner snow peak with a steep final slope. Deo Tibba (6,001 m / 19,688 ft) has a flat snow-dome summit, but the approach crosses the steep Duhangan Col and a crevassed glacier on fixed ropes. Yunam (6,111 m / 20,049 ft) is non-technical but steep: scree, moraine and snow slopes of about 50° near the top.

Only a little higher, Indrasan (6,220 m / 20,407 ft) is often called the hardest peak of the Pir Panjal, with an icefall, steep ice and a rock band on the final peak. Menthosa (6,443 m / 21,138 ft) is glaciated, graded alpine AD on its normal route: 40–55° snow and ice on the ENE ridge above a crevassed flank.

FAQ

Questions people ask about mountaineering terms

What is a col in mountaineering?

A col is a high pass or saddle between two summits. Climbers often camp on a col or cross it; on Deo Tibba in Himachal, the Duhangan Col is the crux of the climb.

What is a bergschrund?

A bergschrund is the crevasse at the head of a glacier, where moving ice tears away from the ice or rock above. It is often the first obstacle before a face and is usually most open in summer and autumn.

What is a serac?

A serac is a tower or block of glacier ice, formed in icefalls and where crevasses cross. Seracs can collapse at any time of day, so routes avoid lingering below them.

What is the difference between alpine style and expedition style?

Alpine style means the team carries everything and climbs in one push, without fixed ropes or stocked camps. Expedition style uses stocked camps, fixed ropes and support staff.

What does summit push mean?

The summit push is the final climb from the top camp to the summit and back, usually started in the dark with an alpine start, while the snow is still firm.

What do the grades F, PD, AD, D, TD and ED mean?

They are French alpine grades: facile (easy), peu difficile, assez difficile, difficile, très difficile and extrêmement difficile. Kang Yatse II in Ladakh is PD; Menthosa's normal route in Lahaul is about AD.

What is a cornice?

A cornice is an overhanging lip of wind-blown snow on a ridge. It can break off under a climber's weight, so stay well back from the edge.

What is an LO on an Indian expedition?

An LO is the liaison officer the Indian Mountaineering Foundation appoints to every foreign expedition. Teams also pay a US$500 LO equipment hire.
