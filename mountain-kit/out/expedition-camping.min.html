What does base camp mean?

Base camp is the main camp at the foot of a climb: the staging area where the team lives, cooks and acclimatises before moving higher. Above it, bigger peaks add camp 1 or an advanced base, then a small summit camp for the final push. Teams ferry loads up and sleep lower. Merriam-Webster defines a base camp as a place that serves as the starting point or staging area for an expedition, usually stocked with shelter and supplies. On a mountain the idea grows into a ladder of camps: a road-head where the vehicles stop, a base camp where the team settles in, sometimes one or more intermediate camps, and a small summit camp from which climbers leave in the dark for the top.

Most pages that explain base camp are dictionaries, and most pages about expedition camping sell tents. This guide does neither. It explains how a camp system works on climbs in the mountains of India, using the real camp altitudes of Himachal peaks, and covers the parts first-timers ask about most: sleeping warm, safe water, toilets and the rubbish rules of the Indian Mountaineering Foundation (IMF).

Road-head to summit

The camp ladder: base camp, camp 1 and summit camp

  1. Road-head.Where the road ends and the walk begins. Gear is loaded for the walk-in, often with a first night in a meadow camp on the way.
  2. Base camp.The big, comfortable camp at the foot of the climb. The team lives, cooks and acclimatises here, practises snow skills and waits out bad weather.
  3. Camp 1 or advanced base.An intermediate camp on bigger peaks. Teams stock it by carrying loads up and coming back down to sleep, then move up once it is ready.
  4. Summit camp.A small tent camp as close to the top as is safe, used for one or two nights. Climbers leave it before dawn for the summit and return to it, or further down, the same day.

How many camps? A small trekking peak needs only one or two camps. Bigger peaks add camp 1 or an advanced base and a summit camp, each stocked by load ferries from the camp below.

Acclimatisation

Carry high, sleep low: why camps are spaced the way they are

Above about 3,000 m, most climbers follow the rule "climb high, sleep low": they carry a load up to the next camp, leave it there and come back down to sleep. Their bodies adjust to the height they touched during the day, and the higher camp is stocked by the time the team moves up.

The sleeping-altitude rule. The CDC Yellow Book advises that above 3,000 m you should not raise your sleeping altitude by more than 500 m a night, and should add an extra night for every 1,000 m you climb. That is why good itineraries include rest and acclimatisation days at base camp. This page is information, not medical advice: tell your guide at once if you feel unwell.

What the team provides

Life in camp: what the outfitter provides and what you bring

Usually provided on a guided climb

  • Tents at base camp and the high camps.
  • A kitchen and mess tent, and the meals.
  • Ropes and other group climbing gear.

Usually yours to bring

  • A sleeping bag warm enough for the high camp.
  • Your layers for camp evenings.
  • A headlamp and water bottles.
  • Your personal kit from the mountaineering gear list.

Lists differ between climbs, so get yours in writing before you travel. Sleeping bags and down jackets are commonly rented in Manali and Solang if you would rather not buy them; check the condition and fit before you leave town.

Sleeping-bag ratings

Sleeping warm: how to read a sleeping-bag temperature rating

Camps above 5,000 m are well below freezing at night, even in autumn. Most sleeping bags are tested to the ISO 23537-1:2022 standard (formerly EN 13537), on a manikin wearing one layer of long underwear and lying on an insulating mat. The label shows three numbers:

RatingWhat it means
ComfortA standard woman sleeps comfortably in a relaxed position
Lower limitA standard man sleeps for eight hours, curled up, without waking
ExtremeA survival-only rating for a standard woman, with a risk of hypothermia: never plan around it

Choosing a bag. Ask your outfitter how high the top camp is and how cold the nights are expected to be, then pick a bag whose comfort rating covers it. Sleep in dry layers, not in the clothes you sweated in; our guide to what to wear on a Himalayan climb explains the layers.

Water · toilets · rubbish

Water, toilets and rubbish on an expedition

Water

  • Camp water is boiled or treated.
  • Do not drink untreated stream or river water: the Ladakh tourism advisory warns against it.

Toilets

  • Base camps dig deep toilet pits.
  • Where there is no pit, Leave No Trace advises a cathole 15–20 cm (6–8 in) deep, at least about 60 m (200 ft) from water, camps and trails.

Rubbish

  • Under IMF rules, only biodegradable waste may be left, and only below the snow line.
  • Non-biodegradable waste goes down to the road-head in sacks.
  • All ropes and gear are retrieved, with photo proof in the expedition report.

The seven Leave No Trace principles

  1. Plan ahead and prepare.Know the rules of the area and the climb.
  2. Travel and camp on durable surfaces.Use existing camp sites where you can.
  3. Dispose of waste properly.Pack it in, pack it out.
  4. Leave what you find.Stones, plants and old cairns stay where they are.
  5. Minimise campfire impacts.Check the local rules before lighting any fire.
  6. Respect wildlife.Watch from a distance and never feed animals.
  7. Be considerate of other visitors.Shepherds, villagers and other teams share these valleys.

FAQ

Base camp and camping questions

What does base camp mean?

The main camp at the foot of a climb: the staging area where the team lives, cooks and acclimatises before moving higher.

What is the difference between base camp and summit camp?

Base camp is the big, comfortable camp lower down. Summit camp, or high camp, is a small tent camp as close to the top as is safe, used for one or two nights before the summit push.

What are camp 1 and camp 2?

Intermediate camps between base camp and the summit on bigger peaks. Teams stock them by carrying loads up and coming back down to sleep: climb high, sleep low.

How cold is it at high camp?

Well below freezing at night above 5,000 m, even in autumn. Bring a sleeping bag rated for it and dry layers to sleep in.

What sleeping-bag rating do I need?

Look at the ISO comfort and lower-limit ratings. Ask your outfitter how high the top camp is and how cold the nights are expected to be, then choose a bag whose comfort rating covers it.

Where do you go to the toilet on an expedition?

In toilet pits dug at base camp. Higher up, waste is packed out or buried following the rules, and non-biodegradable rubbish is carried down to the road-head under IMF rules.

Is the water safe to drink at camp?

Camp water is boiled or treated. Do not drink untreated stream or river water.

Do I need my own tent on a guided climb?

No. Tents and the kitchen come with a guided expedition. Confirm in writing what is provided and what you bring, usually your sleeping bag and personal kit.
