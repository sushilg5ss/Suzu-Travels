What makes Bhutan different

Bhutan does not chase visitor numbers. It manages them deliberately, which is why the valleys stay quiet, the monasteries are not overrun and the air genuinely feels different. For Indian travellers it is one of the few places abroad that is both easy to enter and completely unlike home. You will not find the crowds of Thailand or the neon of Singapore. You will find fortress monasteries above river confluences, prayer flags on every pass, and a country that measures itself by something other than growth.

On permits and fees: Indian nationals do not need a visa, but Bhutan requires an entry permit for each traveller and applies a Sustainable Development Fee, charged at a concessional rate for Indian and regional visitors. Both the permit process and the fee have been revised more than once in recent years. We confirm the exact current requirement and cost for your travel dates in writing before you pay anything.

Flying in or driving in

By air to Paro

Direct flights operate from Delhi, Kolkata, Guwahati and Bagdogra. Paro’s approach between the mountains is famously dramatic and the airport is served by a small number of certified pilots, so schedules are weather-sensitive. Flying saves two full days compared with the overland route and is the sensible choice on a five-day trip.

Overland via Phuentsholing

The land border at Phuentsholing connects to Jaigaon in West Bengal, reached via Bagdogra or New Jalpaiguri. Permits are processed here. It costs you a day in each direction but it is considerably cheaper and the drive up to Thimphu through the foothills is genuinely beautiful. Good choice if you have seven days or more and want to keep costs down.

About the Tiger’s Nest climb

This is the reason most people come, and the part most underestimate. Taktsang sits about 900 metres above the valley floor. The path is well made but steep and continuous, at altitude, and the round trip takes four to five hours at a moderate pace. Horses can carry you as far as the cafeteria roughly halfway, but the final stretch of stairs must be walked. Reasonably fit travellers of any age manage it comfortably if they start early and take it slowly. If anyone in your group has knee or heart concerns, tell us and we will plan an alternative day rather than have them find out on the mountain.

When to go

March–May · Best overallSpring brings rhododendron and jacaranda into flower, clear skies and comfortable walking weather. Paro Tshechu falls in this window and is worth planning around.
September–November · ExcellentPost-monsoon clarity, the sharpest mountain views of the year and the Thimphu and Punakha festivals. The most popular season, so hotels fill early.
December–February · Cold and quietVery few visitors and crisp visibility. Thimphu and Paro are cold but manageable; high passes can close with snow and Punakha stays warmer than the rest.
June–August · MonsoonGreen valleys and low rates, but cloud, leeches on trails and occasional road disruption. Only worth it if you are flexible and budget-driven.

Common questions about Bhutan

Do Indians need a visa for Bhutan?

No. Indian citizens do not require a visa. You do need an entry permit, which must be arranged before or on arrival, and a Sustainable Development Fee applies at a concessional rate for Indian nationals. We handle the permits and state the current fee clearly in your quote.

Can I travel to Bhutan independently as an Indian?

Independent travel is possible for Indian nationals, but permits, the SDF and the guide requirement for most sites make it considerably more work than it looks. Most travellers find a package simpler and no more expensive once everything is counted.

How many days does Bhutan need?

Five to six days covers Paro, Thimphu and Punakha properly, including the Tiger’s Nest climb. Seven or eight lets you add Phobjikha valley or travel overland. Under four days is only realistic if you fly both ways and skip Punakha.

Is Bhutan suitable for elderly travellers?

Yes, with planning. The valleys, dzongs and drives are comfortable for most older travellers. Tiger’s Nest is the exception — it is a genuine climb. We build the itinerary so that anyone not walking up still has a full day rather than sitting in a hotel.

Will I find vegetarian food?

Easily. Bhutanese cuisine uses a great deal of cheese, chilli and rice, and hotels are used to Indian guests. Jain requirements need advance notice, which we give when booking rather than on the day.

Is mobile network and internet available?

Yes in Paro, Thimphu and Punakha. Indian SIMs do not work on domestic rates, so plan on a local SIM or hotel wifi. Coverage thins on the passes and in Phobjikha.
